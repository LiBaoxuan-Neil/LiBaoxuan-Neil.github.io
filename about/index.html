About

I’m Baoxuan Li. I work at Inovance Technology on backend and platform engineering, with most of my day-to-day practice centered on shared capabilities within InoCube. This work brings me close to the point where infrastructure decisions become contracts that other teams build upon: a boundary that looks convenient locally can become expensive once it is shared, and a small compatibility decision can determine whether a change is easy to adopt or difficult to coordinate.

Over the long term, I have been interested in platform engineering, distributed systems, and cloud-native infrastructure. What connects these areas for me is not a particular technology stack, but the question of how to make a complex system understandable and evolvable. I try to make responsibilities explicit, keep dependencies intentional, and leave room for teams and systems to change without requiring everything around them to move at the same time.

The Problems I Work On

Many platform problems begin as implementation problems and become boundary problems as their scope grows. A shared service may gradually absorb business-specific behavior; a common interface may hide differences that should remain visible; central governance may simplify one workflow while limiting the autonomy of the teams it serves. I spend much of my time identifying where responsibility should live and what a platform should standardize, rather than assuming that more centralization or another abstraction will make the system simpler.

Evolution introduces a second set of constraints. Existing callers have assumptions, deployment environments differ, and coordinated migrations are rarely as tidy as a design diagram suggests. Compatibility therefore needs to be designed alongside the target architecture, so adopters can move at a pace appropriate to their own systems. At the same time, autonomy cannot mean losing common governance, and consistency cannot mean forcing every domain into one implementation. The useful balance is usually a small shared contract with clear ownership around it.

Operability is part of the same design problem. A service boundary is incomplete if failures cannot be located, changes cannot be rolled out safely, or operators cannot tell which layer owns an unexpected behavior. These concerns are why I focus on constraints and trade-offs—the organizational shape of a change, its migration path, and its behavior under failure—rather than treating platform work as a sequence of tools to assemble.

How I Think About Engineering

These considerations have reinforced four principles that I return to when evaluating platform changes:

  • Boundaries before abstractions. An abstraction is useful only after responsibility and ownership are clear; otherwise it can hide coupling while leaving the underlying decisions unresolved.
  • Compatibility is an architectural capability. Stable transition paths let a system evolve without turning every improvement into a synchronized change across callers and teams.
  • Platforms should enable autonomy. Shared contracts and governance should make independent work safer, not pull every domain decision into a central implementation.
  • Operability belongs in the design. Failure isolation, observability, rollout, and recovery need explicit ownership before production behavior tests the architecture for us.

I do not see these as universal rules that remove judgment. They are prompts for asking better questions: which differences are essential, which constraints are temporary, what must remain stable, and who needs to act when the system does not behave as expected. Good engineering decisions are often less about finding a perfect structure than about making the consequences of a chosen structure visible.

About This Blog

I write to preserve the reasoning that is usually lost after an implementation settles. Posts begin with the problem context and its constraints, then examine the trade-offs, the path taken, and what later practice revealed. Recording that process helps me test whether a design still makes sense outside the immediacy of a project, and it gives future changes a more useful starting point than an unexplained final state.

The subjects follow both my work and my current learning: platform and distributed-system design, cloud-native infrastructure, AI-native systems, agent engineering, Go, and Rust. They may look like different areas, but I approach them through the same questions about boundaries, compatibility, autonomy, and operation. This blog is where I work through those questions in public—not to present finished answers, but to make the reasoning behind engineering choices more precise and reusable.

关于我

我是李宝暄，目前在汇川技术从事后端与平台工程相关工作，实践主要围绕 InoCube 共享能力展开。我长期关注平台工程、分布式系统和云原生基础设施。比起某项技术本身，我更关心如何说清责任、控制依赖，让复杂系统保持可理解、可演进，也让不同团队能按各自节奏改变。

我在解决的问题

平台问题常随范围扩大而变成边界问题：共享服务吸收越来越多领域差异，统一接口可能掩盖本应保留的不同，集中治理也可能削弱使用方自治。我关注责任应该落在哪里、平台究竟应统一什么，而不是用更多抽象和工具堆叠代替判断。

演进还受既有契约、部署差异和发布节奏约束。兼容路径要与目标架构同时设计，使调用方可以逐步迁移；自治不能放弃统一治理，统一也不应取代领域自主决策。故障定位、安全发布和异常归属同样要进入设计，因为可运维性也是边界的一部分。

我的工程观

这些思考让我反复回到四项原则：

  • 边界先于抽象。 先明确责任与所有权，再提取共性，否则抽象只会遮住耦合。
  • 兼容性是架构能力。 稳定的过渡路径让系统演进不必依赖跨团队同步。
  • 平台应赋能自治。 公共契约与治理应让各领域更安全地独立演进，而不是将领域决策集中到中心实现。
  • 可运维性参与设计。 故障隔离、可观测、发布与恢复需要明确边界，不能留到上线后补救。

这些原则不是口号，而是提醒我追问哪些差异必须保留、哪些契约需要稳定，以及异常时由谁行动。设计未必完美，但应让选择的后果足够清楚。
